KOC vendor registration: the equipment supplier guide to KOC, KNPC and KIPIC

September 9, 2026Updated October 2, 202611 min readVendor Guides

How an equipment supplier gets registered with Kuwait Oil Company, KNPC and the former KIPIC assets at Al-Zour: the portals, the prequalification route, where CAPT fits, what Law No. 1 of 2024 changed about local agents, and what an Ex device dossier has to contain.

Key takeaways
  • KIPIC was dissolved and merged into KNPC, with completion marked on 1 July 2026, so suppliers reach Al-Zour through KNPC rather than a separate KIPIC registration.
  • One registration does not reliably cover the KPC group; KOC, KNPC and other subsidiaries use different categories, questionnaires and document sets, so treat each separately.
  • Law No. 1 of 2024 lets foreign companies open a Kuwait branch and bid in public tenders without a local agent; registered agencies still need Kuwaiti majority ownership.
  • Hazardous-area devices for Kuwait need three parallel strands: an IECEx certificate, KUCAS product conformity through PAI, and CITRA radio type approval.

Kuwait packs an unusual amount of classified-area work into a small country. Almost everything sits in a strip running south from Kuwait Bay: the gathering centres of Greater Burgan, the Ahmadi operational belt, the refinery line from Mina Al-Ahmadi through Mina Abdullah to Al-Zour, and the older petrochemical plant at Shuaiba. Almost all of it is bought by one family of state companies. If you manufacture equipment for hazardous areas, and that includes something as small as a Zone 1 handset, your route to that market runs through supplier registration with Kuwait Oil Company and Kuwait National Petroleum Company before it runs through anybody’s sales team.

A great deal has been written about Kuwaiti tendering for drilling contractors and EPC firms. Very little of it is written for equipment manufacturers, whose applications are read differently, whose product categories are routinely misfiled, and whose technical dossier has a second life inside an operator engineering review long after procurement has finished with it. This guide covers the mechanics as they stand in 2026, including a structural change to the sector that has caught out suppliers whose paperwork still names a company that no longer exists.

Who you are actually registering with

Everything commercial in Kuwaiti oil and gas sits under Kuwait Petroleum Corporation, the state holding company. Below KPC the subsidiaries divide the work: Kuwait Oil Company runs upstream production, gathering and gas handling; Kuwait National Petroleum Company runs the refineries and local fuel marketing; Petrochemical Industries Company runs the Shuaiba petrochemical and fertiliser plants; Kuwait Gulf Oil Company runs the Kuwaiti half of the Partitioned Zone with Saudi Arabia; KUFPEC operates overseas; and Kuwait Oil Tanker Company moves the product.

The structural change matters. Kuwait Integrated Petroleum Industries Company, KIPIC, which built and ran the Al-Zour refinery, LNG import terminal and the planned petrochemical complex, was dissolved and merged into KNPC. The oil minister marked the completion of that merger on 1 July 2026, and the combined company now oversees roughly 1.4 million barrels a day of refining capacity across Mina Al-Ahmadi, Mina Abdullah and Al-Zour. Practically, that means there is no longer a separate KIPIC vendor registration to chase. Suppliers who want to reach Al-Zour go through KNPC. If your capability statement, your covering letter or your reference list still addresses KIPIC as a standalone customer, it dates you immediately, and in a market where the buyer is trying to work out whether you actually follow the sector, that is an avoidable own goal.

The KOC vendor registration route

KOC runs its own supplier-facing system, the KOC eBusiness Portal at ebusiness.kockw.com. Anyone can browse it without registering, which is the first thing a serious supplier should do, because the portal is unusually candid about what is coming. It publishes requests for proposal at several stages of life, from future planned through announced, issued, under evaluation and awarded; it publishes requests for quotation; and, most useful of all for a manufacturer, it publishes prequalification programmes with their work categories, separated into contractor prequalifications and manufacturer prequalifications.

Read that manufacturer prequalification list before you fill in anything. It tells you what categories KOC currently recognises and how it words them, which is the single biggest determinant of whether the right buyer ever sees your profile. Registration itself is the familiar shape: create a company profile, enter corporate details, upload documentation, then submit against the categories you are claiming.

Three requirements come up consistently in Kuwaiti registrations and are worth preparing before you start rather than after a rejection. The first is a quality management certificate; ISO 9001 from an accredited body is treated as a baseline rather than a differentiator. The second is a genuine health, safety and environment package: a policy, a management system summary, and multi-year incident statistics. Kuwaiti buyers read the HSE section carefully, and a two-page boilerplate policy is conspicuous. If you are selling safety equipment, an unconvincing safety management story is corrosive even where it is not formally disqualifying. The third is document attestation. Corporate documents from outside Kuwait are expected to be legalised through the Kuwaiti embassy in the country of origin, with Arabic translation where required. That step is administrative rather than difficult, but it has a queue attached to it, and it is the most common reason a Kuwaiti application sits unmoved for weeks.

KNPC prequalification and the K-Tendering portal

KNPC operates its own eSourcing registration, reached through the company website under suppliers and contractors. Alongside it, KPC runs a group-level platform, the K-Tendering portal at ktendering.com.kw, which carries KPC and its subsidiaries including KNPC, KOC, KUFPEC and PIC.

The trap here is assuming that one registration covers the group. It does not, reliably. Suppliers routinely find that a profile approved by one subsidiary does not carry across to another, and that the categories, the questionnaire and the document set differ. Treat each entity you genuinely want to supply as a separate exercise, sequence them rather than running them in parallel while your documents are still in draft, and keep one master document pack so that the fifteenth upload is not a fresh scavenger hunt.

Prequalification, once you are registered, is where a manufacturer earns the right to be invited. It is a technical exercise: what you make, to which standards, with what production and inspection controls, with which references. Limited tenders in Kuwait are by invitation, and the invitation list is drawn from prequalified suppliers in the relevant category. Being registered but not prequalified is a common and frustrating middle state.

Where the Central Agency for Public Tenders fits

Not everything runs through the oil companies’ own portals. The Central Agency for Public Tenders, CAPT, was established under the Public Tenders Law, Law No. 49 of 2016, and sits under the Council of Ministers. It runs public tendering on behalf of most government bodies above a value threshold set by the law, and it maintains registers of suppliers and contractors. For an equipment manufacturer selling to the oil sector, CAPT is usually adjacent rather than central, but two provisions of the Public Tenders Law reach into oil-sector contracts and shape how you should structure a bid.

The first is a local content obligation: a foreign contractor is required to buy a defined proportion of its contractual requirements from the local market or from local suppliers registered with CAPT, where those are available. The second is a price preference for locally produced goods. Both are widely quoted at specific percentages, and both have been amended over the life of the law, so confirm the figures that apply to your tender rather than relying on a number from a blog, including this one. The strategic point survives whatever the current percentages are: a bid assembled with a Kuwaiti supply, service and spares component is structurally stronger than an identical bid shipped entirely from abroad.

The local agent question, and what Law No. 1 of 2024 changed

This is the part of Kuwaiti market entry where the received wisdom is now out of date, and where getting it wrong costs a supplier either a needless 51 per cent partnership or a needlessly stalled application.

Historically, a foreign company could not conduct business in Kuwait except through a Kuwaiti agent or a Kuwaiti partner holding at least 51 per cent. Law No. 1 of 2024, published in the Official Gazette on 21 January 2024, amended Article 24 of the Commercial Law and Article 31 of the Public Tenders Law. A foreign company may now establish a branch in Kuwait and commence its operations without a local agent, and foreign bidders may participate in public tenders without the previous local agent and commercial registration requirement.

That is a genuine liberalisation and it changes the calculus for a manufacturer that intends to build a long-term Kuwaiti position. It does not, however, abolish the commercial agency framework. Commercial agencies and distributorships are still governed by the Commercial Agencies Law, Law No. 13 of 2016, and an agency arrangement registered with the Ministry of Commerce and Industry still requires a Kuwaiti national or a company with majority Kuwaiti ownership. The two regimes now coexist, and the practical question for an equipment supplier is which one you actually want.

For most device manufacturers the answer is still a Kuwaiti partner, but for commercial rather than legal reasons. Somebody has to hold stock inside the country, present spares within a working day of Ahmadi, take a warranty claim, attend a technical clarification meeting at short notice and maintain the relationship with the operator’s instrumentation and telecoms groups between purchase orders. A branch can do that; a partner usually does it sooner and cheaper. What has changed is that this is now a business decision rather than a legal obligation, and you can make it on the merits.

What an Ex device dossier needs in Kuwait

Vendor registration gets you seen. The technical dossier is what gets a device carried past the gate, and for hazardous-area electronics the operator engineering review is a separate and later hurdle from anything procurement does.

Three strands run in parallel, and none substitutes for the others.

  • Ex certification. An IECEx Certificate of Conformity is the document a Kuwaiti hazardous-area engineer actually reads, generally alongside an ATEX certificate for buyers who work to the European scheme. What matters is the complete equipment marking string, not a marketing claim. For a handheld device intended for process areas the meaningful target is Zone 1 and Zone 21, gas group IIC, with the temperature class stated. Buyers searching for an explosion proof mobile phone or a flameproof mobile phone are usually, in certification terms, looking for an intrinsically safe device with an Ex ib protection level, and it is worth being explicit about that in your submission rather than letting the terminology blur.
  • National product conformity. Regulated products entering Kuwait clear under the Kuwait Conformity Assurance Scheme, KUCAS, operated by the Public Authority for Industry. Conformity procedures are completed before shipment through a certification and inspection body approved by PAI, which issues a Technical Evaluation Report covering the model and a Technical Inspection Report covering the consignment.
  • Radio type approval. Because a phone is a radio, it needs type approval from CITRA, the Communication and Information Technology Regulatory Authority. Approval is generally granted on review of test reports produced to recognised standards rather than requiring local testing, and certificates are issued for a fixed term. Confirm the current term and the current submission route with CITRA, because the type approval regulation has been revised more than once.

Two Kuwait-specific technical points are worth anticipating in the dossier rather than answering later. Ambient temperature is the first: an Eastern Kuwait summer sits far above the 40 degrees Celsius reference ambient that many certificates assume, so the certified ambient range and the temperature class will be checked, and a device that is only rated to a standard ambient invites a question you would rather not receive during evaluation. Dust is the second: fertiliser, polymer and bulk handling at Shuaiba put Zone 21 alongside Zone 1, so a device covering gas only is a partial answer to an integrated site.

Product coding, and where device suppliers get filed wrong

The most avoidable failure in Kuwaiti registration is not rejection. It is invisibility. Buyers search the registered supplier base by category, and hazardous-area electronics sits awkwardly across several of them. A Zone 1 handset can plausibly belong under communications equipment, under electrical equipment for hazardous areas, under safety equipment, and under IT hardware, and the buyer looking for it may search any of those depending on which department raised the requisition.

Register against every category that genuinely applies rather than the single one that feels most natural, and include your accessories, chargers and, if you offer them, device management and support services. Then check how you appear from the outside. A manufacturer filed under one broad code is absent from the other four searches, and no amount of follow-up email fixes a database problem.

What stalls Kuwaiti registrations

From watching hazardous-area suppliers work through this, the same five things account for most of the delay, and none of them is fatal.

  1. Documents that are not legalised through the Kuwaiti embassy, or not translated where translation is required.
  2. Financial statements older than the latest closed year, or unaudited where audited accounts are expected.
  3. An HSE submission with a policy but no incident statistics and no management system evidence.
  4. Category selection that does not match the actual scope of supply, which produces either a rejection or a silence.
  5. Ambiguity about the legal entity: a branch half-established, or an agency arrangement discussed but not registered, so the buyer cannot tell who would hold the contract.

On costs and timelines, be sceptical of any specific figure you read, including from advisers. Portal registration itself is not where the money goes; the cost is internal, in the weeks of work assembling audited accounts, insurance, quality and HSE evidence and getting documents attested. Practitioners commonly describe the end-to-end process as taking months rather than weeks. Treat that as indicative only, and confirm current fees, forms and timelines directly with the KOC eBusiness Portal, the KNPC eSourcing registration and CAPT before you plan a launch around them.

Specifying the device itself

Registration is procedural. The specification is where a device supplier either fits the site or does not. In Kuwait the demand pattern is consistent: gathering centre and plant operators who need one device instead of a radio, a camera and a paper round sheet; refinery turnaround crews at Mina Abdullah and Mina Al-Ahmadi where the contractor population multiplies for a few weeks; sour gas facilities in the north where hands stay free and detection stays on; and marine and jetty operations at Al-Zour and Shuaiba.

Buyers reach that requirement through search terms rather than certification codes. Enquiries arrive as explosion proof mobile phone, ATEX mobile phone, ex phone, zone 1 mobile phone, and most often of all as some version of explosion proof mobile phone price in Kuwait. The terminology is loose but the underlying need is precise: a handset that can be carried into a Zone 1 area with a certificate behind it, managed from the same enterprise mobility console as the rest of the estate, and supported locally when one is dropped down a stairwell. Frequently asked questions

Do you need a Kuwaiti agent to bid for KOC or KNPC tenders?

Not as a legal requirement since Law No. 1 of 2024, which lets foreign bidders take part in public tenders without the previous local agent and commercial registration requirement. Commercial agencies under Law No. 13 of 2016 still require a Kuwaiti national or majority-Kuwaiti company. Many device makers still choose a Kuwaiti partner for stock, spares and warranty support.

What documents does KOC expect from an equipment manufacturer?

Three requirements come up consistently. A quality management certificate, with ISO 9001 from an accredited body treated as a baseline. A genuine HSE package with a policy, management system summary and multi-year incident statistics. And legalisation of foreign corporate documents through the Kuwaiti embassy in the country of origin, with Arabic translation where required, which is the most common cause of delay.

Where does KOC publish prequalification opportunities?

On the KOC eBusiness Portal at ebusiness.kockw.com, which anyone can browse without registering. It lists requests for proposal from future planned through to awarded, requests for quotation, and prequalification programmes split into contractor and manufacturer categories. Reading the manufacturer prequalification list first shows which categories KOC currently recognises and how it words them.

What role does CAPT play for oil sector suppliers in Kuwait?

The Central Agency for Public Tenders runs public tendering for most government bodies above a value threshold set by law and keeps supplier registers. It is usually adjacent for oil-sector equipment sales, but the Public Tenders Law adds a local content obligation for foreign contractors and a price preference for locally produced goods. Confirm the current percentages for each tender.
