JSRS Oman registration: the supplier route to PDO, OQ and the Omani energy sector

September 9, 2026Updated October 2, 202611 min readVendor Guides

How the Joint Supplier Registration System works, why PDO and OQ will not deal with a supplier without an active certificate, what it costs and how long it lasts, how in-country value is applied in Oman, and what an Ex device dossier needs.

Key takeaways
  • Since 1 July 2021, suppliers bidding for Omani oil and gas operators must hold an active JSRS certificate, and keep it active for the full contract tenure.
  • JSRS buyers search by JSRS Category Code, so hazardous-area electronics suppliers should claim every JCC code that genuinely applies, including accessories, chargers and services.
  • A JSRS certificate makes a supplier eligible to work with PDO, but a separate technical evaluation of the Ex certificate, marking string and protection level follows.
  • OQ8, which operates the Duqm refinery, is a fifty-fifty joint venture between OQ and Kuwait Petroleum International and a legal entity separate from OQ.

Oman does something the rest of the Gulf does not. Instead of leaving every operator to run its own duplicate supplier prequalification, the sector runs one shared registry, and a supplier who is not current in it does not get to bid anywhere. That registry is the Joint Supplier Registration System, JSRS, and it is the single most useful thing to understand about selling equipment into Oman, because it converts what would otherwise be a dozen separate approval campaigns into one.

Plenty of material exists on JSRS for contractors and service companies. Very little of it is written for equipment manufacturers, whose applications are categorised differently and whose product dossier has a second life inside an operator technical review long after registration is complete. This guide covers the mechanics as they stand in 2026, the in-country value framework that sits alongside them, and what an Ex device dossier has to contain for an Omani buyer.

What JSRS is and who runs it

JSRS is Oman’s industry-wide supplier certification system, implemented by Business Gateways International on behalf of the Ministry of Energy and Minerals. It launched in 2014 as an in-country value initiative for the Omani oil and gas industry, and it functions as both a compliance gate and a marketplace: operators and contractors search it, and suppliers are visible in it by category.

The compliance half became hard rather than advisory on 1 July 2021. From that date, the Ministry of Energy and Minerals mandated that all suppliers participating in any bidding activity floated by the oil and gas operators and concession companies must hold an active JSRS certificate. Contractors and suppliers awarded contracts and purchase orders must hold an active certificate for the entire tenure of the contract, not merely at bid submission. That second clause is the one that catches suppliers out: a certificate allowed to lapse mid-contract is a contractual problem, not an administrative one.

The operator community using the system includes Petroleum Development Oman, Shell Development Oman, BP Exploration (Epsilon), Oman LNG and OQ Exploration and Production, among roughly twenty operators, with a much wider population of contractors sourcing through it. JSRS also publishes headline figures for registered suppliers and catalogued products; treat those as marketing rather than as procurement intelligence, and judge the system by the operator list instead.

The registration process and the JCC category code

Registration itself is not complicated. You submit the registration form with company details and capabilities, accept the order confirmation, receive credentials, pay the subscription fee, and the Business Gateways validation and accounts teams review the submission before the account is activated and the dashboard opens. The document checklist is the usual pack: corporate registration, financials, insurance, quality and health, safety and environment evidence.

What actually determines whether registration earns you anything is category coding. JSRS uses its own taxonomy, the JSRS Category Code or JCC, developed to map products and services precisely to the oil and gas industry, replacing the general-purpose classification the system previously mapped to. Buyers search on it.

For hazardous-area electronics this is the step where suppliers quietly disappear. A Zone 1 handset can plausibly belong under communications equipment, under electrical equipment for hazardous areas, under safety equipment and under IT hardware, and the person raising a requisition may search any one of them depending on which department owns the budget. Claim every JCC code that genuinely applies, including accessories, chargers and any device management or support service you provide. Then look at your own record the way a buyer would, using the search terms they would use. A manufacturer filed under one broad code is absent from the other searches, and no amount of follow-up fixes a taxonomy problem.

The system is also structured in tiers, with a national business framework layer, the JSRS certification platform itself and an optional layer of additional industry certifications on top. Understand which tier your intended activity actually requires before you buy anything more than you need.

Fees and validity

JSRS is unusual among Gulf registration schemes in publishing its fee structure openly, which makes planning easier.

For Omani national companies the subscription is banded by company classification, using the small and medium enterprise classification framework, with micro, small and medium bands at the low end and a substantially higher band for large companies. A separate band applies to companies that are not classified under the SME framework. International suppliers pay a flat fee with a choice of a one-year or a three-year subscription, the three-year option carrying a discount against three single years.

Published figures at the time of writing put the Omani bands between roughly 50 and 900 Omani rials depending on classification, and the international subscription at roughly 1,000 US dollars for one year or 2,250 US dollars for three, with value added tax applying to Omani companies. Treat those numbers as indicative rather than current: fees are revised, and the authoritative figure is the one shown on the JSRS portal when you register. Certificate validity for Omani national suppliers is three years; international suppliers hold whichever term they subscribed for.

The real cost, as everywhere, is internal. Assembling audited financials, insurance with the right scope, and health, safety and environment documentation that reads as a real management system rather than a policy statement is where the weeks go. Omani operators, and Petroleum Development Oman in particular, read the HSE section attentively, and a supplier of safety equipment with an unconvincing safety management story creates an impression that is hard to undo later.

How PDO uses JSRS

Petroleum Development Oman is the largest single buyer in the Omani upstream and the reason most suppliers approach the country at all. Its position is stated plainly: all vendors and suppliers of products and services who wish to do business with PDO should hold a valid JSRS certificate. JSRS is the prerequisite, not the whole journey, and holding a certificate makes you eligible to be engaged rather than guaranteeing that you will be.

What follows for an equipment manufacturer is a technical evaluation against PDO’s own requirements, which for hazardous-area electrical equipment means the certificate, the marking string and the equipment protection level rather than the datasheet. Plan for two timelines running in parallel: the registration timeline, which you can start immediately, and the technical acceptance timeline, which depends on an engineering function inside the operator and moves at its own pace.

PDO’s operating environment shapes the specification more than any document does. The interior fields are reached by long desert roads, and at a remote station in Block 6 a failed handset is not replaced until the next logistics run. Thermal and chemical enhanced recovery at Qarn Alam and Mukhaizna combines hot surfaces, hydrocarbon vapour and heavy maintenance traffic. Self-sufficiency, sealing and battery endurance carry more weight in an Omani evaluation than they do in a market where stores is a five-minute walk away.

OQ, OQ8 and the rest of the operator community

OQ is the state integrated energy group and covers upstream through to refining, petrochemicals and marketing. Its exploration and production arm sources through JSRS alongside the other operators, and OQ entities in the downstream and logistics chain use JSRS-certified suppliers for procurement.

Two OQ-related assets deserve separate attention because they are frequently misattributed.

  • Sohar. The refinery and the surrounding petrochemical and industrial estate at Sohar sit within OQ’s downstream operations, alongside Sohar Port and Freezone with its own industrial population. Turnaround work here brings in a large contractor population issued equipment for the duration of a job, which is exactly the profile that suits a centrally enrolled and remotely wipeable device fleet.
  • Duqm. The refinery at Duqm is operated by OQ8, a fifty-fifty joint venture between OQ and Kuwait Petroleum International, with a nameplate capacity around 230,000 barrels per day, located in the special economic zone at Duqm. It is a distinct legal entity from OQ, and a supplier who assumes an OQ relationship reaches Duqm automatically is making an assumption worth checking.

Beyond those, Oman LNG operates the liquefaction trains and jetty at Qalhat near Sur, and runs its own supplier registration process alongside JSRS. As always, decide which specific operators you are targeting and check how each of them actually buys.

In-country value in Oman

Oman’s in-country value framework is older and less centrally scored than the Emirati or Qatari equivalents, but it is embedded in tender evaluation rather than bolted on. Integration is the responsibility of the Ministry of Energy and Minerals, which works with operators to ensure ICV clauses are written into contracts and scopes of work, and an ICV committee bringing together government and industry provides governance. The sector has developed standard ICV terms and conditions and standard metrics so that bidders are evaluated consistently, with a defined set of elements factored into the evaluation criteria and a reporting mechanism attached.

Two features matter more for an equipment supplier than the scoring itself. The first is Omanisation: employment and development of Omani nationals is a live obligation, monitored, not merely a stated preference. The second is the requirement to use small and medium enterprises and local community contractors, which is enforced through the contracts management system and reaches down into how a prime contractor structures its own supply chain.

It is also worth knowing that Oman’s state audit authority has publicly flagged inconsistencies in how oil and gas companies apply the ICV programme. The honest reading of that for a supplier is that the requirement is real but its application varies by operator and by contract, so the practical approach is to read the ICV clauses in the specific tender rather than to plan against a general national rule.

For a device manufacturer, the localisation options are better than they first appear. A device fleet generates configuration, enrolment, staging, training, warranty handling and repair work over years. Placing that work with an Omani partner, with Omani technicians trained to do it, is a genuine local contribution rather than a paper one, and it is what a well-constructed Omani bid looks like.

The Ex device dossier for Oman

Registration and ICV are commercial gates. Three technical ones run alongside them.

  • Ex certification. The IECEx Certificate of Conformity is the document an Omani hazardous-area authority actually assesses, usually alongside an ATEX certificate where the buyer works to the European scheme. What is read is the complete equipment marking string: equipment group and category, gas or dust, protection concept, gas group, temperature class, equipment protection level and the certified ambient range. For a handheld device intended for process areas, Zone 1 and Zone 21 with gas group IIC is the meaningful target.
  • Product conformity. Conformity for regulated goods entering Oman is handled through the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion and its standards and metrology function. Confirm the current requirement for your product category before shipping anything, evaluation samples included.
  • Radio type approval. Radio and telecommunications equipment must be type approved by the Telecommunications Regulatory Authority, and consignments need a TRA import permit to clear customs. Both are applications an Omani registered supplier makes, which is another reason to have the local entity in place before the first shipment rather than after the first order. TRA amended its type approval regulations in 2024 and, in March 2026, introduced a self-declaration of conformity service that replaces mandatory test reports for eligible equipment and shortens review timelines. If your compliance advice on Oman predates that change, get it refreshed, because it may be describing a more onerous route than the one now available.

Two Oman-specific technical notes belong in the dossier rather than in a later clarification. Certified ambient range is the first: interior summer conditions sit well above the reference ambient many certificates assume, and an evaluator will check the range and the temperature class against the area classification. Dust is the second: mineral, polymer and bulk handling around the industrial ports puts Zone 21 alongside Zone 1, so a gas-only specification answers half the question at an integrated site.

One terminology point. Buyers search for a flameproof mobile phone or an explosion proof mobile phone, but for a handheld the protection concept that applies is intrinsic safety, an Ex ib equipment protection level. Use the search term in marketing if you like; do not use it in a technical submission where the certificate says something else.

What stalls JSRS applications

  1. Category coding that is too narrow, so the supplier is registered but invisible to the searches that matter.
  2. Certificates allowed to lapse mid-contract, which breaches the requirement to hold an active certificate for the whole tenure.
  3. Health, safety and environment documentation that is a policy rather than a management system, with no incident statistics behind it.
  4. Financial statements older than the latest closed year, or unaudited where audited accounts are expected.
  5. Assuming JSRS is the whole approval, and not budgeting time for the operator technical evaluation that follows it.
  6. An Ex certificate whose zone coverage or certified ambient range does not match the areas the device is being proposed for.

Specifying the device itself

Oman spreads its hazardous work thinly across a very large country, and that changes the priorities. Field operators at Yibal and Fahud cover long routes and need one device instead of a radio, a camera and a paper round sheet. Enhanced recovery facilities need a handset that carries the permit, the isolation record and the photograph without a return trip to the office. LNG train and jetty work at Qalhat needs something that takes a washdown and holds a call from a berth. Turnaround scopes at Sohar and Duqm need a fleet of loaned devices that can be audited when the crew demobilises.

Those buyers arrive through plain search terms rather than certification codes: flameproof mobile phone, explosion proof mobile phone, ATEX mobile phone, ex phone, zone 1 mobile phone, and most often some version of explosion proof mobile phone price in Oman. The vocabulary is loose and the requirement is precise: a handset that can legitimately be carried into a Zone 1 area with a certificate behind it, managed from the enterprise mobility console the telecoms team already runs, and supported by someone who can get a replacement to an interior station. Frequently asked questions

How much does JSRS registration cost for an international supplier?

Published figures at the time of writing put the international subscription at roughly 1,000 US dollars for one year or 2,250 US dollars for three years. Omani national companies pay banded fees of roughly 50 to 900 Omani rials by classification, plus VAT. Fees are revised, so the authoritative figure is the one shown on the JSRS portal at registration.

How long is a JSRS certificate valid?

Certificate validity for Omani national suppliers is three years. International suppliers hold whichever term they subscribed for, choosing between a one-year and a three-year subscription, with the three-year option discounted against three single years. Suppliers awarded contracts or purchase orders must keep an active certificate for the whole tenure of the contract, not just at bid submission.

Does a phone need TRA type approval to be imported into Oman?

Yes. Radio and telecommunications equipment must hold type approval from the Telecommunications Regulatory Authority, and consignments need a TRA import permit to clear customs. Both applications are made by an Omani registered supplier. In March 2026 TRA introduced a self-declaration of conformity service that replaces mandatory test reports for eligible equipment and shortens review timelines.

Is in-country value scored in Omani oil and gas tenders?

Yes, though less centrally than in the UAE or Qatar. ICV is embedded in tender evaluation through standard terms, conditions and metrics, with the Ministry of Energy and Minerals and an ICV committee providing governance. Omanisation and the use of SMEs and local community contractors are monitored obligations. Application varies by operator and contract, so read each tender's ICV clauses.
