Explosion proof, intrinsically safe and flameproof: what the three words actually mean

September 9, 2026Updated October 2, 202611 min readCompliance

Buyers search for an explosion proof mobile phone and almost always need an intrinsically safe one. Here is what each protection concept means, why a handset is Ex ib rather than Ex d, and how to write the specification so the right device answers it.

Key takeaways
  • Explosion proof and flameproof are two regional names for one technique, containment under IEC 60079-1, which suits wall-mounted equipment rather than handsets.
  • Intrinsic safety under IEC 60079-11 limits energy so that ignition cannot occur, and it is the concept every credible hazardous area handset uses.
  • Level ia stays safe with two faults and suits Zone 0, ib with one fault suits Zone 1, and ic, safe in normal operation, suits Zone 2.
  • Check the equipment protection level at the end of the marking string: Gb means Zone 1 capable, while Gc means the device is Zone 2 only.

Every month, buyers looking for a handset they can legitimately carry into a refinery, a gas plant or a paint shop type explosion proof mobile phone, flameproof mobile phone and intrinsically safe phone into the same search box, on the assumption that the three phrases name one product. They do not. They name three different engineering ideas, and only one of them is realistically available in something you can put in a pocket.

The distinction is commercial, not academic. A purchase specification that asks for an “explosion proof phone” is loose enough that a Zone 2 rugged handset and a Zone 1 intrinsically safe handset can both answer it, and the cheaper one usually wins. A specification that names the zone, the equipment protection level and the acceptable protection concepts cannot be answered by the wrong device at all. This article is written to get you from the first kind of specification to the second.

Three words, three different ideas

Explosion protection has never had one method. It has a family of recognised techniques, each of which attacks the ignition triangle from a different direction, and each of which is written up as a separate part of the IEC 60079 series. Three of those techniques account for nearly all the confusion.

Flameproof, marked Ex d, is containment. The standard is IEC 60079-1. The idea is that you accept that an explosive mixture will get inside the enclosure, and you accept that the electrical parts inside may ignite it. You then build the enclosure so that it survives the internal explosion without rupturing, and so that every joint and shaft opening is a long, tight, precisely machined gap. That gap, the flamepath, cools the escaping gases below the ignition temperature of the surrounding atmosphere before they reach it. Containment and cooling, not prevention.

Explosion proof is the North American name for the same idea. In the National Electrical Code, NFPA 70, an explosionproof apparatus is defined in essentially those terms: an enclosure able to withstand an explosion of a specified gas or vapour occurring within it, and to prevent ignition of the atmosphere around it. So “explosion proof” and “flameproof” are two regional names for one technique. If you say ex proof phone or exproof phone in an enquiry, this is what the words mean on their face, even though it is almost never what the enquirer wants.

Intrinsic safety, marked Ex i, is prevention. The standard is IEC 60079-11. Instead of building a fortress around an ignition-capable circuit, you design the circuit so that it can never become ignition-capable in the first place. Voltage, current, stored energy in capacitors and inductors, and component surface temperatures are all limited so that no spark or hot spot the equipment can produce carries enough energy to ignite the specified gas. There is nothing to contain, because there is nothing capable of igniting anything.

The full family of protection concepts

It helps to see the whole set at once, because a hazardous area phone conversation touches at least four of these.

Concept Marking Standard The idea in one line
Flameproof, explosion proof Ex db, Ex d IEC 60079-1 Let it ignite inside, contain it, cool the escaping gas
Intrinsic safety Ex ia, Ex ib, Ex ic IEC 60079-11 Limit energy so ignition is not possible
Increased safety Ex eb, Ex ec IEC 60079-7 Engineer out arcs, sparks and hot surfaces in normal operation
Encapsulation Ex ma, Ex mb, Ex mc IEC 60079-18 Pot the circuit in compound so the atmosphere cannot reach it
Pressurisation Ex pxb, Ex pyb, Ex pzc IEC 60079-2 Hold clean air or inert gas inside at positive pressure
Restricted breathing Ex nR IEC 60079-15 Seal the enclosure well enough that gas ingress is negligible
Dust protection by enclosure Ex ta, Ex tb, Ex tc IEC 60079-31 Keep dust out and keep the surface cool

One historic marking still turns up on datasheets and in old site records: Ex nA, non-sparking equipment, from the “type of protection n” standard IEC 60079-15. It no longer exists in that standard. When IEC 60079-15 moved to its fifth edition, Ex nA was withdrawn and its requirements were absorbed into IEC 60079-7 as level of protection Ex ec. Ex nA and Ex ec describe the same level of protection, and if you are reading a specification or an equipment register that still says nA, treat it as ec. Energy-limited nL became Ex ic under IEC 60079-11 by the same logic. This matters when you compare two competing quotations that use different vintages of language for the same Zone 2 capability.

Why an explosion proof phone is not a real product

Flameproof telephones exist. They are perfectly ordinary industrial products, bolted to a wall or a pole beside a pump house, and if you look at the specification sheets you will see why the concept does not scale down to a handset. A typical Ex d industrial telephone is a cast aluminium or stainless box of roughly 200 by 330 by 130 millimetres. The published weights sit somewhere between about 7 and 12 kilograms. That mass is not decoration. It is what a certified flamepath and an enclosure rated to survive an internal explosion actually costs in metal.

Now list what a smartphone has to expose to the outside world: a touchscreen the user has to press through, a microphone port, a speaker port, a camera window, at least one charging connector, and buttons. Every one of those is an opening in the enclosure, and under the flameproof concept every opening has to become a certified flamepath or a separately certified sealed assembly. You would be building a machined pressure vessel with a glass face, and you would still have to deal with a lithium cell sitting inside it. The result would weigh several kilograms, cost far more than the phone, and no one would carry it up a set of stairs to a sample point.

Intrinsic safety scales down beautifully by comparison, because a phone is already a low-energy device. A handset runs at a few volts from a single cell. The engineering work is real, and it is not trivial: the certification body assesses spark ignition and thermal ignition for every circuit, the battery and its protection circuitry get specific attention, and the assessment is done under fault conditions rather than in normal operation only. But it is achievable in a device the size and weight of a normal phone, which is precisely why every credible hazardous area handset on the market is an Ex i device.

So when a datasheet advertises an explosion proof phone for Zone 1, what is behind it is an intrinsically safe phone. The marketing word and the certificate word have simply drifted apart.

ia, ib and ic: the part buyers skip

Intrinsic safety is not one thing. IEC 60079-11 defines three levels, and the difference between them is how many faults the design has to survive while still being incapable of ignition.

  • Ex ia stays safe with any combination of two countable faults applied simultaneously. It carries equipment protection level Ga and is the only level suitable for Zone 0.
  • Ex ib stays safe with one fault. It carries EPL Gb and is suitable for Zone 1.
  • Ex ic is safe in normal operation. It carries EPL Gc and is suitable for Zone 2.

A higher protection level always satisfies a lower requirement, so an Ex ia device can be used in Zone 1 and Zone 2, and an Ex ib device can be used in Zone 1 and Zone 2 but not Zone 0. This is the single most useful thing in the whole marking string, and it is the thing most often lost when a specification is written in marketing language. Two handsets can both be sold as an ex mobile phone. If one is Ex ic IIC T4 Gc and the other is Ex ib IIC T4 Gb, they are not competing products, they are equipment for two different areas of your plant.

The Zone 2 trap

A large share of the rugged phones marketed as hazardous area phone products are certified to Zone 2 and Zone 22 only, usually through increased safety at level ec. That is a legitimate certification and a genuinely useful product. It is simply not a Zone 1 device, and Zone 1 is where field operators, maintenance crews and inspection teams actually spend their day on most process plants.

The check takes five seconds once you know where to look. Find the equipment protection level letters at the end of the marking string. Gb means Zone 1 capable. Gc means Zone 2 only. If the marking says Ex ec or Ex nA, it is a Zone 2 device whatever the brochure headline says. If a supplier quotes a zone 1 mobile phone and the marking on the certificate resolves to Gc, the quotation does not meet the requirement and the discrepancy belongs in the tender clarification, not in a site incident report two years later.

Reading the marking string

A European marking for a Zone 1 gas handset looks like this:

II 2 G Ex ib IIC T4 Gb

Read left to right. II is the equipment group, meaning surface industry rather than mining. 2 is the ATEX equipment category, which corresponds to Zone 1 for gas. G says gas rather than dust; a device covering both will carry a second string ending in D. Ex introduces the type of protection. ia is intrinsic safety at the two-fault level. IIC is the gas group, the most demanding one, covering hydrogen and acetylene atmospheres as well as everything in IIA and IIB. T4 is the temperature class, a maximum surface temperature of 135 °C. Gb is the equipment protection level, confirming Zone 1.

Alongside that string you should also see the ambient temperature range, an IP rating, the certificate number, and on ATEX equipment the CE mark with the four-digit number of the notified body that oversees production quality. Why the words diverged, and why search reflects it

The vocabulary split along regional lines and then leaked. In North America, explosionproof enclosures are the flagship Division 1 technique for motors, junction boxes, light fittings and control stations, so “explosion proof” became shorthand in the field for “safe to use where there is gas”. In parts of South Asia and the Gulf, the everyday term for the same idea is “flameproof”, and enquiries for a flame proof mobile arrive meaning nothing more specific than a phone for a hazardous area. In Europe, the ATEX directive put the word ATEX on everything, so buyers ask for an ATEX mobile phone or an ATEX phone when what they mean is a certified device, whatever the concept.

None of this is a mistake worth correcting in conversation. It is worth correcting in documents. Search language and specification language are allowed to be different, and a supplier who understands both is easier to work with than one who lectures you about terminology.

What to write on a purchase specification

Here is a specification skeleton that cannot be answered by the wrong device. Replace the bracketed values with the ones from your own hazardous area classification drawing.

  1. Area classification. The device shall be suitable for [Zone 1] gas and [Zone 21] dust as classified under IEC 60079-10-1 and IEC 60079-10-2, or for [Class I, Division 1, Groups C and D] where the site is classified under NEC 500.
  2. Equipment protection level. Minimum EPL [Gb] for gas and [Db] for dust. Devices marked Gc or Dc only are not acceptable.
  3. Gas and dust group. Gas group [IIC]. Dust group [IIIC].
  4. Temperature class. [T4] or better, with the certified ambient temperature range stated and covering [minus 20 to plus 55] °C.
  5. Protection concept. Intrinsic safety to IEC 60079-11 at level [ia or ib]. Increased safety at level ec, non-sparking nA, and restricted breathing nR are not acceptable for this area.
  6. Certification. A current IECEx Certificate of Conformity and, for equipment placed on the European market, an ATEX EU-type examination certificate. For sites in the United States, listing for the classified location by a nationally recognised testing laboratory.
  7. Documentation. The certificate schedule, all specific conditions of use where the certificate number carries an X suffix, the declaration of conformity, and the instruction manual, supplied before award.
  8. Marking. A photograph of the actual product label, to be checked character by character against the certificate.

Two lines of that list do most of the work. Line 2 kills the Zone 2 substitution. Line 5 kills the vocabulary problem, because it names the concepts rather than the marketing category. If you write nothing else differently, write those two.

Where a Class I Division 1 phone fits

Buyers on American-code sites ask for a Class I Division 1 phone, and the mapping is not quite one to one. Zone-marked equipment is accepted in Division 2 locations under the cross-acceptance rules in NEC Article 500, but a Division 1 location generally calls for equipment listed for Division 1. That is why a manufacturer aiming at both worlds runs two certification programmes rather than one, and why an ATEX or IECEx certificate alone will not close out a US Division 1 requirement. The short version

Explosion proof and flameproof are the same technique under two regional names, they mean containment, and they belong to wall-mounted equipment rather than pocket equipment. Intrinsic safety is energy limitation, it is what every credible hazardous area handset uses, and its three levels decide which zone the device may enter. Write the zone, the EPL and the concept into your specification, and the terminology problem disappears.

Frequently asked questions

Is there such a thing as an explosion proof mobile phone?

Not in the literal sense. Under the flameproof concept every opening must become a machined flamepath or a separately assessed sealed assembly, and typical Ex d industrial telephones weigh about 7 to 12 kilograms. A smartphone has a touchscreen, ports, a camera window and buttons, so the concept does not scale down. Phones sold as explosion proof for Zone 1 are intrinsically safe devices.

What replaced the Ex nA marking?

Ex nA was withdrawn when IEC 60079-15 moved to its fifth edition, and its requirements were absorbed into IEC 60079-7 as level of protection Ex ec. The two describe the same level of protection, so treat nA as ec when reading old specifications or equipment registers. Energy-limited nL became level ic under IEC 60079-11 by the same logic.

Can a Zone 2 rugged phone be used in Zone 1?

No. Many rugged phones marketed as hazardous area products hold certification for Zone 2 and Zone 22 only, usually through increased safety at level ec. If the marking says Ex ec or Ex nA, or the protection level resolves to Gc, it is a Zone 2 device whatever the brochure says, and it does not meet a Zone 1 requirement.

Does an ATEX or IECEx certificate cover a Class I Division 1 location?

No. Zone-marked equipment is accepted in Division 2 locations under the cross-acceptance rules in NEC Article 500, but a Division 1 location generally calls for equipment listed for Division 1. That is why a manufacturer aiming at both systems runs two certification programmes, and why an ATEX or IECEx certificate alone will not close out a US Division 1 requirement.
